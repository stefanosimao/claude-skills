# Ficus Bonsai (genus Ficus — common species: F. microcarpa/retusa "Ginseng," F. benjamina, F. religiosa, F. rubiginosa)

## Physiology & Dormancy

Ficus is tropical/subtropical and does not undergo true seasonal dormancy the way temperate species (maples, junipers) do. Tropical species such as Ficus rarely enter true dormancy at all. This has a major practical consequence: total leaf drop in a ficus is almost always a stress/shock response (cold, draft, sudden light change, relocation, under/overwatering) rather than a normal seasonal cycle. Don't reassure a worried owner with "it's just going dormant" — investigate the actual cause instead.

Ficus store substantial energy reserves in trunk and root tissue and can push new growth from old wood and even bare, leafless branches. This is why ficus has a deserved reputation as one of the most forgiving and recoverable bonsai species — but it also means appearance is a poor proxy for vitality. Always defer to the scratch test and root inspection (see Diagnostic Framework in main SKILL.md) over visual impression.

Ficus produces a white latex sap from cuts, which is normal and harmless to the tree; it can be stopped by dabbing the cut with a wet finger if it's bothersome.

## Watering

- Water when the top inch (top ~2-3cm) of soil has dried, not on a fixed calendar — water every 5 to 7 days, or when the top inch of soil is dry, generously whenever the soil gets slightly dry, adjusting for pot size, climate, and season.
- Ficus is relatively tolerant of occasional watering mistakes compared to many bonsai species — it can tolerate occasional over or underwatering — but tolerance is not immunity; chronic overwatering still causes root rot, and prolonged drought still kills roots.
- Humidity matters more than most owners expect, especially indoors near heating. Misting helps but can cause fungal issues if overdone in stagnant air; a humidity tray is generally a safer steady-state solution than frequent misting.
- Never let a ficus sit in standing water — drainage is non-negotiable, more so than for many other plants people are used to caring for.

## Light & Temperature

- Wants bright light, ideally several hours of direct or near-direct sun — a south-facing window indoors is ideal; east/west is acceptable with supplementation; north alone usually requires a grow light.
- Comfortable temperature range: nighttime lows above 60°F/15°C are ideal for active growth; exposure to temperatures below 40°F (4°C) can cause significant damage, including leaf drop, dieback, or stunted growth.
- Keep away from cold drafts, AC vents, and heating vents — both can dry out the root zone and shock the tree even when ambient room temperature seems fine.
- Can be moved outdoors in summer once nights are reliably warm, which often produces a vigor boost; reintroduce to indoor conditions gradually before cold weather, not abruptly.
- A sudden, deliberate environment change (new house, rotating the pot, moving rooms) commonly triggers a full leaf drop-and-regrow cycle even when nothing is "wrong" — this is a known ficus quirk, not necessarily a symptom requiring intervention.

## Common Failure Modes (diagnostic specifics for this species)

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden total/near-total leaf drop after a move, rotation, or draft | Environmental shock | Ficus leaves are highly sensitive to light-direction and intensity changes; the tree sheds and regrows leaves adapted to new conditions |
| Leaves pale, curl, then yellow/brown over days-to-weeks | Often overwatering | Check soil moisture retention and root health before assuming underwatering |
| Leaves brown and crispy, soil bone-dry | Underwatering/desiccation | Distinguish from rot via root inspection — dry brittle roots vs. mushy dark roots |
| Sticky residue or small folded/curled leaves with movement inside | Pests (scale, mealybugs, thrips, spider mites) | Pests like scale and spider mites can affect Ginseng Ficus Bonsai trees; thrips specifically cause leaf-folding with visible larvae inside the fold |
| Soil constantly wet, foul smell, mushy dark roots | Root rot | Most dangerous failure mode; requires root trim + repot, not just "water less" |
| Slow overall decline despite correct watering/light | Possible nutrient deficiency or pot-bound roots | Check fertilizing schedule and whether repotting is overdue |

Note: well-maintained ficus is comparatively low-risk for pests/disease — if a Ficus bonsai is properly cared for, infestation with diseases or pests will almost never occur, and bright, warm placement with adequate nitrogen fertilization further reduces aphid risk. Persistent pest issues are often a signal of an underlying stress (low light, poor air circulation) rather than bad luck.

## Pruning & Repotting

**Pruning:**
- Prune during the growing season (spring through early fall) for structural work; light maintenance trimming can be done year-round.
- Common rule of thumb: prune back to 2 leaves after 6-8 leaves have grown to maintain density and shape.
- Ficus back-buds readily from old, even leafless wood, which makes it forgiving of harder pruning than many species tolerate — strong cuts necessary for trunk thickening don't affect the ficus's health, and new shoots will grow from old wood.
- Seal larger wounds (generally >1cm) with cut paste to support healing.
- Use sterilized, sharp tools — clean cuts heal faster and reduce infection risk.

**Wiring:**
- Branches are flexible and easy to wire, but bark can mark or get bitten into quickly as the branch thickens — check wires regularly and remove/rewire before they cut in.

**Repotting:**
- Typical interval: every 2–3 years for younger/vigorous trees, stretching to every 3–5 years (or longer for very established trees) as the tree matures — repot every 2-3 years for younger trees and every 3-5 years for older, more established trees, though the real trigger should be root-bound soil rather than the calendar alone.
- Best timing: spring, once nighttime temperatures are reliably warm — repotting when nighttime temperatures consistently exceed 60°F (15°C) encourages active growth and minimizes transplant shock.
- Root pruning: ficus tolerates this well; as a conservative default, avoid removing more than about a third of the root mass in one session to protect recovery capacity.
- After repotting: keep warm, sheltered, moderate (not harsh) light, and increased humidity; hold off on fertilizing for several weeks until new growth confirms the roots have re-established (see Fertilizing).
- Don't combine repotting with heavy pruning or relocation in the same window — stack stresses one at a time.

## Fertilizing

- Feed during the active growing season, roughly spring through early autumn; benefit from regular feeding every two to four weeks during their growing season, using either liquid feed at label-recommended strength or slow-release organic pellets.
- Reduce or stop in winter unless the tree is kept warm and well-lit enough to keep growing — in that case feed sparingly rather than at full strength.
- Wait 4–8 weeks after repotting (or until new growth confirms root recovery) before resuming fertilizer — feeding stressed, newly-cut roots risks burn.
- Never fertilize a visibly stressed, pest-infested, or recovering tree — nutrients won't help and may compound root stress; restore health first, feed second.

## Quick-reference defaults (use when the user wants a simple answer, not a deep dive)

- Water: top inch of soil dry → water thoroughly until drainage runs out the bottom.
- Light: brightest spot available, ideally a south-facing window.
- Temperature: keep above 60°F/15°C; never below 40°F/4°C.
- Fertilize: every 2–4 weeks spring–fall, stop or reduce in winter.
- Repot: every 2–3 years in spring, or when root-bound.
- If it suddenly drops all its leaves: don't panic — check for shock causes (move, draft, light change) before assuming death; do the scratch test if worried.
